# Chapter 8: Set the Table

Sunday afternoon. Maren stood in the loading dock with the vendors gathered around her, the smell of cheese still rising off the stones. Two notices on the door. The plaza empty. The fair stolen.

"I'm not asking for a wish," she said. "I'm asking for help. Real help."

They listened while she told them the plan. The supper that should have been the fair. A long table on the plaza, open to everyone, serving the last of Elena's bread and tomato soup and whatever anyone brought to share. She wasn't asking permission — the market was closed. She was asking for the table itself.

The Rind stirred in her pocket. "I could fix the injunction," it whispered. "One crumb of bread. One word —"

"No," Maren said out loud. The vendors looked at her. "No more wishes. I'm done fixing things with magic."

Priya moved first. She unpacked her tea cart from under a tarp, despite the notice, and wheeled it onto the plaza. Omar found the long folding table from storage — the one from step eleven of the plan — and dragged it into the sun. Nico brought sawhorses and planks of good wood and spent an hour leveling it with a tool Maren had never seen him use before.

She watched him work. He tested the table's weight with both hands, then sat on the edge to make sure it would hold. Methodical. Careful. The kind of person who built things to stay built.

"There's a chair for you at this table?" he asked, not looking up.

"There's a chair for everyone," Maren said. "That's the whole point."

Nico nodded. He ran a thumb along the wood grain. "Good," he said. "I had a feeling this one might matter."

The candles came out of Omar's tin. The soup went into Elena's oldest pot, the one from 1962. The bread went on the table in baskets. Maren was ladling broth when Ms. Fenn turned the corner, holding a folded document, her heels clicking against the plaza stones.

"A public gathering on this block is prohibited," she said. "Mr. Hargrove has filed an injunction."

"On a dinner?"

"On this dinner. He's holding his own event at five — champagne for the investors, and the first swing at six." Ms. Fenn set the document down on the table between the bread baskets. "You understand what he's doing, don't you? He's not trying to stop you. He's trying to make sure no one is here when it goes."

She left. The vendors stood around the long table. The candles were unlit. The soup steamed. And a sheriff's car pulled up to the plaza just as Priya reached for the first match.
