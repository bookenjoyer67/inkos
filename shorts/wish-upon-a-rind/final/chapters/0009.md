# Chapter 9: The Almost-Wish

The sheriff was a patient woman named Deputy Correa. She stood by her car, arms folded, and read the injunction like the weather.

"I understand," she said, not unkindly. "But you can't hold a public gathering on this block. The owner filed a formal order. I have to enforce it."

The plaza went quiet. The candles stayed unlit. The soup pot sat in the center of the table, steaming, and the table — the whole beautiful plank table Nico had built — stood empty. The vendors who had started to drift back had stopped at the edge of the plaza, watching the sheriff's car like a wall.

A voice came from Maren's pocket. Steady. Old.

"One wedge left, child. One word. You could wish this all away." The Rind's voice was lighter than she remembered, worn thin. "You could end it. The injunction, the demolition, all of it. One sentence. You don't have to trust anybody. You don't have to ask."

The wedge was in Maren's hand before she knew she'd reached for it. Warm, golden, light. The bread was in her other pocket — a piece of the loaf from this morning, still soft at the center.

One wish. The last one.

The words were in her throat, beating like a second heart. I wish the market were safe. I wish the demolition would stop. I wish —

She looked down the plaza at the vendors waiting at the edges. At Priya with her hand frozen over an unlit match. At Omar, cradling the old folder like a child. At Nico, standing beside the table he'd built with his own hands, and the look on his face was not hope.

It was trust. He was waiting to see what she would do.

"What would Grandma do?" Maren asked.

The Rind's voice was soft. "She would set the table."

Maren put the wedge back in the tin. She closed the lid.

"I'm not wishing," she said, and her voice came out strange and strong in the quiet plaza. "I'm setting the table."

She picked up the soup pot — heavy, burn-scarred — and carried it out to the table herself. Deputy Correa watched. Maren set the pot down. She lit the first candle.

"Nobody leaves this market hungry."

Behind her, a match struck. Priya lit her tea cart's lantern. Omar lit the second candle. Nico pulled out a chair, the wood scraping against the stones, and sat down at the table as if he'd been waiting his whole life for permission.

Then a vendor came. Then another. Then the crowd was moving — not pushing, just flowing — past the sheriff's car, which had become scenery, and into the plaza where the table waited.

"The injunction," Deputy Correa said, but she didn't unfold her arms. She stood there, watching the candles come alive, and then she said, almost to herself, "I'm not on duty until six."

The table began to fill.

From her pocket, the Rind was humming Elena's song — the one she hummed while she cooked, the one that meant the bread was almost ready.

"You almost wished," it said.

"I know."

"It would have worked."

"Then I wouldn't have learned anything." Maren ladled soup into a bowl and set it at an empty place. "And the market would have been saved tomorrow by the same woman who broke it yesterday. That's not enough. That's just more of the same."

The Rind was quiet. Then it said, "She would be proud, mija," and the old word made Maren's eyes sting.

She set another bowl at the chair at the end of the table — the one no one had claimed yet — and went to find the next person who needed a place.
