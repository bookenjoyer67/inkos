# Chapter 10: The Dinner Stays

Rex Hargrove arrived at six with a bottle of champagne in one hand and a sledgehammer over his shoulder. Ms. Fenn followed with a briefcase.

He stopped at the edge of the plaza. The long table was full. Candles burned. Soup steam rose. Baskets of bread traveled hand to hand. Children drew on the ground with chalk. Someone had strung market-lights from the lampposts, glowing like a row of small suns.

"This is a gathering," Hargrove said. His voice sounded strange. "There's an injunction."

"Good evening, Mr. Hargrove." Maren had been watching for him. She was calm in the way a person is calm after making peace with something. "We saved you a seat."

She pulled out the chair at the head of the table. The empty one. A bowl of soup sat in front of it, still steaming.

"You brought champagne," Priya said from the tea cart. "That's festive. We'll use it for the soup."

Hargrove didn't move. The sledgehammer stayed on his shoulder.

"I'm not here to sit down," he said. "I'm here to —" He stopped. He had seen the photograph.

It wasn't by the window now. Someone had taken down the cracked frame, repaired the glass, and propped it at the center of the table beside the soup pot. A boy at a counter. A sign in the background. A promise on the wall.

"The demolition," Hargrove said, thinner now, "is scheduled."

"The city revoked occupancy," Maren said. "The block is legally dead. You don't need to demolish anything tonight." She stepped toward him, not too close. "You can let the table have one night first."

"Then why are you having dinner in a corpse?"

"Because the table's still warm." Maren met his eyes. "That's what Elena used to say. The table's still warm, so the house is still a home."

Hargrove's jaw tightened. He looked over at Ruth's stall — dark, shuttered, the old milk can cracked, the faded sign. Then Omar stepped forward, holding a brown leather ledger, edges soft as cloth.

"This was in the market archives. I almost threw it out in '99."

He laid it open on the table in the candlelight. Hargrove's name was there — REX, in a child's printing, in the margin of a page dated fifty years ago. Ruth Hargrove's stall. First week free. Sponsorship by Elena Vasquez. And a note in Ruth's careful handwriting:

Rex promised to keep the table set.

The sledgehammer lowered. It rested on the ground, and Hargrove held it like a crutch.

"That's my grandmother's handwriting," he said, and his voice cracked near the end.

From Maren's pocket, so softly only she could hear, the Rind murmured, "Ruth would have wanted a seat at this table."

Maren pulled out the chair at the head of the table again.

"Sit down, Rex," she said. "There's soup. There's bread. There's a photograph of you when you were eight and still believed in things." She set a fresh bowl where the empty one had gone cold. "That's all this is. A dinner. You can eat it and go break the market in the morning, and I'll stand right here and not stop you."

"You should have left this buried," Hargrove said, and his voice was not angry anymore.

"I didn't dig up your past, Rex. I set a place for it."

He looked from the photograph to Ruth's stall to the long table. His hand came off the sledgehammer. His fingers found the edge of the wood — the same plank table Nico had built, steady, true, built to hold. For a long moment, nobody breathed.

Then Rex Hargrove sat down.

A beat of silence. Then Priya said, very steadily, "I'm putting your champagne in the soup," and the laughter cracked the moment open like bread. Hargrove didn't laugh, but he picked up the spoon, and he did not put it down.
