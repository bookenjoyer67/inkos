# Chapter 1: Out of Crumbs

Maren screamed. The cheese did not scream back, which somehow made it worse.

It sat on a small wooden board inside the tin, a wedge of pale gold, speckled, with a rind so dark it looked almost black. It had the shape of a slice of pie and the mild, unimpressed expression of a man waiting for a bus.

"You're not real," Maren said.

The cheese sighed. "I am the most real thing in this room, child. Please close the tin. The light is harsh."

She slammed the lid. Counted to ten. Opened it again.

"Better," said the cheese. "I am the Rind. Your grandmother made me. She had a gift for fermentation and a terrible habit of keeping promises. You are Maren. You are about to lose this stall, your cart went through a pothole, and your rent is due in nine days. Sit down and listen."

Maren sat because her legs made the decision for her. Her eyes went to the tin. Eight wedges, exactly like the one speaking, arranged in a circle like the numbers on a clock.

"Eight wedges," the Rind said. "Seven are for wishing. The eighth is spoken for."

"Spoken for by what?"

"By the end of your story. You'll know it when you get there."

Maren ran her thumb over a wedge. She had two dollars in her pocket, a cart with a cracked axle, and a landlord who sent rent reminders in all caps. The demolition notice was still folded in her jacket.

"How does it work?"

"One wish per day. One wedge per wish. The wedge must be eaten with bread." The Rind's voice dropped, almost fond. "The wish will be granted exactly as it is spoken. Literally, permanently, and with my personal mischief included. You will not like how precise I can be."

"What happens if I don't wish?"

"Then you never touch the wedges, and the tin stays here, and the block comes down around it." The Rind paused. "But you will wish, Maren. You are your grandmother's blood, and she never once let a table go empty when she had bread to put on it."

Maren found a heel of bread at the bottom of her bag. Two days old, going hard at the edges. It was all she had.

She broke off a wedge of the cheese, laid it on the bread, and ate it in two bites. The flavor hit her like a memory — warm butter, the year her grandmother taught her to flip a sandwich. She swallowed.

"I wish I could pay the rent."

The Rind gave a small, satisfied shiver. "Wonderful. That's going to be fun."

A knock rattled the stall door. Maren opened it to find no one. On the ground sat a white envelope with her name on it, the paper so crisp it could have cut her.

Inside was a check for exactly two months' rent, written in careful blue ink. Behind it, a letter: the Pine Street Market had been selected for relocation assistance. Accepting the funds constituted the tenant's official consent to the demolition schedule.

"Consent," Maren read aloud. The word curdled in her mouth.

"You wished to pay the rent," the Rind said pleasantly. "You didn't wish to read the fine print."

The woman who appeared in the doorway wore a gray suit and the smile of a calculator. "Ms. Vasquez," she said. "I'm Ms. Fenn, from Hargrove Development. I see you've received our welcome package."

"I didn't sign anything."

"You didn't have to. Cashing it binds you. The language is the most standard in the industry." Ms. Fenn's smile sharpened just slightly. "Congratulations. You're the first tenant to accept relocation. The countdown has begun."

She left. Maren stood in the cold stall, holding the check, the cheese, and the shape of a mistake that was already bigger than the building above her.

She hurled the tin at the dumpster. It clattered against the metal, paused, and rolled back to bump her shin. The lid fell open. The Rind sat unharmed among its siblings.

"Seven wedges left," it said. "Six wishes, and the one that's spoken for. You're down to six wishes and a mystery, child, and the demolition clock is already ticking."
