# Chapter 3: A Very Perfect Plan

Wednesday morning, Maren woke with her eyes swollen and a plan-shaped hole where hope should have been. She got out of bed, unwrapped the Rind from its napkin, and laid it on the kitchen counter beside a slice of toast.

"One more," she said. "I know what I'm doing this time."

"You said that yesterday."

"I wish I had a perfect plan to save the market."

She broke the wedge over the toast and ate it while the Rind shivered, and a sheet of golden parchment rolled out of the tin like a tongue. Handwriting she recognized — Elena's, loops and all, twelve numbered steps.

Reopen the stall. Cook Elena's menu. Rename nothing. Invite everyone. Find the photograph. Serve the menu at Friday's anniversary supper. Steps seven through eleven covered dough, butter, volunteers, the position of Priya's tea cart, and a folding table that had been in storage since 1962.

Step twelve: Serve the Rind to the one who needs it.

Maren read it twice. "What does that mean?"

"It means the plan ends where I begin," the Rind said. "That's the part you won't like. That's the part your grandmother liked best."

The stall reopened at nine. Maren scrubbed the shelves, replaced the flaking sign, wiped rust off the old scale. Omar brought a box of recipe cards. Priya showed up with tea, a griddle, and opinions about sandwich construction. By noon the smell of browning butter was drifting down the aisle, and people who hadn't visited in years were slowing to sniff.

The photograph was in a box behind the pickle barrels — the same box that had held the tin. Maren wiped the frame with her sleeve. A dusty old picture, sun-faded, bright. A boy sat at Elena's counter, maybe eight, swinging his legs, a half-eaten grilled cheese in both hands. Behind him, through the window, hung a printed sign: FIRST WEEK FREE. NOBODY STARTS WITH NOTHING EXCEPT A TABLE.

The sign belonged to a dairy stall across the way. RUTH'S.

Maren stared at the boy's narrow face. She knew that face. It had been in the demolition notices, the city council hearings, the business section of the Herald. It had gotten colder every year.

"That's Rex Hargrove," she said. "As a kid. At my grandmother's counter."

The Rind sat beside the photograph, quiet. "Look closer."

On the wall of Ruth's stall, tacked beneath the sponsorship sign, was a handwritten note in small careful letters: My grandson promised me the table stays set. — R.H.

"We can't use this," Maren said, her voice thin. "It's private. It's his grandmother."

"You knew he was in the photo, then?"

"I didn't. Did you?"

The Rind was silent for a breath. Then it said, softly, "I knew he was only a boy once. What a waste."

Maren looked from the photo to the stall, already golden with afternoon light, smelling like her grandmother's kitchen. The plan was twelve steps long. The plan was going to work. The plan was going to drag Rex Hargrove's buried past out into the open and set it in a frame.

It felt like carrying a blade she didn't know which way it would cut.

She tucked the photograph under her arm and closed the stall at sunset. Five wedges in the tin now, four of them wishes, one spoken for. Eleven steps done. Twelve to go.
