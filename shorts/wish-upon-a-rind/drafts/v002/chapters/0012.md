# Chapter 12: The Last Wedge

Monday morning came up warm and golden over the plaza. The market smelled of coffee and fresh bread and the faint ghost of melted cheese that would linger for weeks no matter how hard they scrubbed the loading dock.

Maren stood at the long table — the same one, still standing, still full of places — and unwrapped the tin.

The vendors had gathered. Priya wheeled the tea cart into its morning spot. Omar carried a box of historic documents to hand over to the city archive. Council Member Tran had come back with a camera crew because the story had grown too large to leave alone.

The last person to arrive was Rex Hargrove. He came down the aisle in a canvas apron over a collar and jacket, carrying a bowl of tomatoes from a farm he'd driven to at dawn. He set them on the table. He sat down. He had a place now.

Maren opened the tin. A single wedge lay inside, pale gold, its rind soft. It had been waiting all week for this moment.

"The last wedge," Maren said. "You said it wasn't for a wish."

"It wasn't."

"What is it for?"

The Rind sat at the center of the table on its small board, diminished, its voice the same as always — old, warm, entirely unimpressed.

"You wished for enough," it said. "You asked what it meant. You learned what it meant. You spent the week packing that lesson into a table." It gave a small, dry chuckle. "Now you get to eat it. You wished for enough. I am giving it to you. Now I can be finished."

The wedge rose into the air, gently, and began to grow.

It unfolded like a loaf of golden light, spreading across the center of the table — one enormous grilled cheese, brown at the crust, molten within, cut into segments by no visible hand. Steam rose. Butter ran. It covered the length of the long table, and the smell was the year Maren learned to flip a sandwich, and the stall, and the market, and everyone who had ever sat down.

"Eat," the Rind said. "All of you. That's the whole instruction."

Maren broke off a piece, crisp crust, melted center. She brought it to her lips and tasted something she had been reaching for all week: enough.

The table came alive. Vendors passed the great golden sandwich hand to hand, breaking off pieces, passing them down the length of the wood. Nico ate his with his eyes closed. Priya took hers and laughed and cried at once. Omar held his piece and looked at it for a long moment before eating it slowly, like saying goodbye.

The last piece reached Rex Hargrove. He took it. He held it the way you hold something made just for you, just in time. He ate it.

And the Rind — the small, pale, warm presence at the center of the table — sighed, and faded, and was gone.

No flash. No drama. It simply stopped being there, the way a song ends, the way a breath is finished.

Maren looked at the empty space where it had been. She opened the tin one more time.

Inside, folded in her grandmother's handwriting:

No take-backs.

Maren laughed. Something in her chest broke loose and settled into place. Around her, the market was full. The fair that had been stolen was happening anyway, in every direction, not as a single event but as a life.

Nico stayed after the crowd drifted. He leaned against the back of a chair, looking at the scrubbed-clean tin.

"So," he said. "What's the stall called now?"

The sign was already taking shape in her mind. "Enough & Co."

"That's good." He worked at a hangnail. "You going to need a carpenter for the new sign? For the counter? For the shelf that's still unlevel?" He met her eyes. "I'm planning to stay through all of it, if that's not too forward."

Maren looked at him. Sawdust in his hair. Hands that had built a table strong enough to hold a market — and, it turned out, strong enough to hold him too.

"I have enough," she said. "But you can have some, if you want."

He smiled. He reached out and took her hand over the empty tin where the cheese had lived and taught and finished its work.

Behind them, the plaza filled with light. The wind moved through the market's stalls. Somewhere down the aisle, Rex Hargrove was pounding a sign into the ground outside Ruth's old dairy — a fresh wood sign with clean paint, and the words FIRST WEEK FREE, for whoever needed them next.

The fair went on. The table stayed. And nobody left the market hungry.
