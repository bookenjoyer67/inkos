# Chapter 2: Same Page

Maren told the truth at the Tuesday meeting because hiding it felt like drowning.

The vendors gathered in the loading bay at sunrise, a ring of folding chairs and thermoses. Priya came with her tea cart still steaming, arms crossed. Nico had sawdust in his hair and a level sticking out of his back pocket. Omar ran the meeting from a clipboard older than everyone in the room.

Maren read the letter out loud. Silence. Then everyone talked at once.

"You cashed a demolition check?"

"Someone tricked her —"

"Fair doesn't matter. Legal does."

Maren pressed her palms against her eyes. Breathe, she told herself. Fix it. A basket of rolls sat at the center of the table, leftover from a baker's morning delivery. She grabbed one without thinking, tore it open. The Rind warmed her jacket pocket like a stone in the sun.

"You got quiet fast," Priya said. "What aren't you telling us?"

Maren didn't answer. She fumbled a wedge from the tin — small, wrapped in wax — pressed it into the roll, and ate it under cover of the argument. The cheese tasted like guilt and warm butter. She swallowed.

"I wish we were all on the same page," she said, too loud, trying to cut through the noise, "so you could all see it from my side."

The room went quiet. Paper rustled overhead. A single sheet drifted down into Omar's coffee cup, then another, then dozens, settling into laps and jam jars and onto the table.

Every page was identical. A retroactive clause added to every lease in the Pine Street Market: any tenant consent to relocation shall apply to all co-tenants in the shared block. Maren's signature at the bottom.

Omar read it twice. His glasses slid down his nose. "This says one tenant's acceptance binds all tenants. You signed one thing. Now we're all on it."

"But I didn't sign —" Maren's hand went to her mouth.

The Rind hummed, muffled by her jacket, thoroughly pleased. "You wished for the same page, dear. Literally. I do love this part."

The meeting dissolved into accusation, then planning, then a thin, desperate silence. Omar broke it by sliding a folder across the table. Inside, a yellowed form stamped PROVISIONAL in faded red ink.

"Provisional historic-preservation listing," he said. "The market was flagged for protection in 2009. The listing was never activated. If it's activated before demolition, the block can't be touched."

"Then activate it."

"We need a council review, a vote," Omar said, "and proof the market is alive. The application requires an actively operating stall. Your grandmother's stall has been dark since she died. If it's still dark by Friday, the block is theirs."

At the edge of the circle, Priya's face changed. "Tran," she said.

"Council Member Tran drinks tea at my cart every morning. He keeps asking about the demolition rumors." She looked at Maren, something almost like hope in her voice. "If we can show him the market is alive, he could push the review through."

Maren took the brass key Omar offered. It was old, worn smooth by decades of turns. "We reopen Elena's stall by Friday."

"By Friday," Omar agreed, "or the block is theirs. And Maren?" He looked at her over the folder. "Whatever magic you've been doing, stop doing it on us."

The meeting broke up. Maren stood alone at the table, the key warm in her hand, the tin heavy in her pocket. Six wedges left now. Five wishes, and the one that was spoken for.

The Rind's voice came low beneath the noise of the market waking up. "You're learning to count the cost. I do love a student."

"I'm not a student," Maren said. "I'm in over my head."

"You're exactly where you need to be," the Rind said. "Now go open that stall before Friday eats you alive."
