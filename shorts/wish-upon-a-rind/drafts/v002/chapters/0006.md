# Chapter 6: Cheese River

Saturday morning, the smell of cheese had gone from wonder to catastrophe. The loading dock was an amber swamp. The cheddar bulldozer had sagged into a monstrous, creamy stack with a small sign still visible in its ruined middle: PROGRESS IS A GRILLED CHEESE.

The health inspector had come back with two trucks and a fine. The cheese had gotten into the storm drains. The environmental department had been notified. Maren's phone was a ledger of increasingly sharp messages.

Priya found her at the edge of the dock at dawn, crouched before the mess. She sat on a dry crate and watched the amber river creep toward the market door.

"You meant well," she said finally.

"I meant to fix it. I keep making it worse."

"The bulldozer's still blocked. He can't get in."

"He doesn't need to get in," Maren said. "He just needs to stand back and let the city do the job for him. There's a demolition permit sitting in his office, Priya. And an inspection failure. And a foundation that's about to flood."

She stood up, pulled the Rind from her jacket, and looked at it. Two wedges left in the tin. One wish, and the one that was spoken for.

"I can wash it away," she said. "She'd want me to clean up my own mess."

"I'm going to wish on it again," Maren said. "That's the only way."

The Rind, patient as a stone, waited.

"You didn't bring any bread," it said.

Maren had a roll in her bag, flattened from sitting on it. She tore it open, pressed the wedge into the middle, and said, "I wish it would rain and wash all this away."

The Rind made a small, terrible sound. "That's going to be even more fun."

It rained. A single compact thundercloud appeared over the market and emptied itself purely onto the loading dock — not the street, not the roof, not the flower boxes. A perfect rain, aimed at exactly one spot.

The cheese river multiplied. It flooded. It poured into the drains, backed them up, and turned the street into a warm amber slick that smelled like a fondue and then like a leak and then like the kind of heat that makes milk turn.

By noon the city had posted a notice in the market's window. OCCUPANCY REVOKED. NO GATHERING. The drainage influx had weakened the foundation of the oldest wall in the block — the one that had stood since 1922, the one Elena's Table leaned against.

Omar stood beside it, reading the second notice. He took off his glasses and wiped them slowly.

"Hargrove filed an imminent hazard declaration," he said. "Demolition official for Sunday evening. The fair's closing hour." He looked at Maren. "He wanted the fair to be the last thing the block remembered. Now he's going to shut the fair down too."

Behind them, voices rose. Someone said she'd made everything worse. Someone said she'd meant well. Someone else said a cheese river doesn't care what you meant.

Maren sat on the curb with the tin in her lap. One wedge left that could wish. The other one waiting. She started to say the words — I wish I could save the market — and stopped with the first syllable on her tongue.

The Rind's voice was curious. "Go on. I love this part."

Maren's hand stayed at her mouth. "Not yet," she said. "I need to understand first."
