# Chapter 12: The Last Wedge

Monday morning. Sunlight thick as honey over the plaza. The market smelled of coffee and fresh bread and the faint memory of melted cheese that would linger for weeks, no matter how many times they scrubbed the loading dock.

Maren stood at the long table — the same one, still standing, still full of places — and unwrapped the tin.

The vendors had gathered. Nico was there, and Priya, and Omar. Council Member Tran had come back with a camera crew because the story had gotten too big to ignore. The only person who had been missing was the one who arrived last.

Rex Hargrove came down the aisle wearing a canvas apron over a collar and jacket, carrying a bowl of tomatoes from a farm he’d driven to at dawn. He set them on the table without a word. He sat down. He had a place now.

“The last wedge,” Maren said to the Rind, when the table was full. “You said it wasn’t for a wish.”

“It wasn’t.”

“What is it for?”

The Rind sat in the center of the table on its small board. It looked smaller now, worn down, its golden color faded to something almost pale. But when it spoke, its voice was the same as always: old, warm, completely unimpressed.

“You wished for enough,” the Rind said. “You asked what it meant. You learned what it meant. You spent a week packing that knowledge into a table.” It gave a small, dry chuckle. “Now you get to eat it. You wished for enough. I am giving it to you. Now I can be finished.”

The wedge rose into the air, gently, and began to grow.

It unfolded like a loaf of golden light, spreading across the center of the table, a single enormous grilled cheese, brown on the outside, molten and rich where a spatula had pressed it. It covered the length of the long table, steaming, crusted with butter, cut into square segments without any visible hands doing the cutting. Maren smelled the year her grandmother taught her to flip a sandwich. She smelled the stall, the market, the old pickle barrels, the warmth of the bread.

“Eat,” the Rind said. “All of you. That’s the whole instruction.”

The first piece came to Maren. She took it with both hands. The crunch when she bit down was so loud it turned heads.

The table came alive.

Vendors passed the great golden sandwich hand to hand, breaking off pieces, passing them down the length of the table. Nico ate a piece with his eyes closed. Priya took hers with tears leaking out the corners of her smile. Omar held his piece in one hand, and for a moment he just looked at it, and then he ate it slowly, like saying goodbye.

The last piece came to Rex Hargrove. He took it. He held it the way you hold something that was made for you and you only just now found out. He ate it.

And the Rind — the small, remaining, pale wedge at the center of the table — sighed, and faded, and was gone. Not a flash of light. Not a dramatic vanishing. It simply stopped being there, the way a song ends, the way a breath is finished.

Maren looked at the empty space where it had been. She felt her pocket — the tin kept its shape. She opened it.

Inside, a folded note, in Grandma Elena’s handwriting:

No take-backs.

Maren laughed. Something in her chest broke loose and arranged itself in a way that let her breathe. Around her, the market was full. The stalls were open. The fair that had been stolen was happening anyway, in every direction at once, not as an event but as a life.

Nico stayed after the crowd drifted. He leaned on the back of a chair, looking at the cleaned-out tin.

“So,” he said. “The new menu. Elena’s Table.”

“I’m renaming it.” Maren ran her thumb over the tin’s lid. “The stall stays as the anchor, Hargrove’s lease says. But it shouldn’t be called a table unless everyone can sit at it.”

“What are you calling it?”

“Enough & Co.” She set the tin down. “Seemed like the whole point.”

Nico was quiet for a moment. Then he said, “You going to need a carpenter for the new sign? And maybe the counter. And the shelf that’s still unlevel. I’m planning to stay through all of it, if that’s not too forward.”

Maren looked at him. Sawdust in his hair. Hands that had built a table strong enough to hold a market.

“I have enough,” she said. “But you can have some, if you want.”

He smiled, and he reached out, and he took her hand over the empty tin where the cheese had lived and died and taught her everything.

Behind them, the plaza filled with light, and the fair went on, and the market stayed.
