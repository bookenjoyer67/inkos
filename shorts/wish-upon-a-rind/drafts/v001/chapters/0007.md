# Chapter 7: Enough Is a Table

Sunday dawn was cold and white and quiet. The market was closed. The fair was supposed to be opening.

Maren sat at Elena’s counter with the tin open in front of her. Two wedges left. Eight had become seven, and seven had become six, and by half-measures and panics and stupid, hopeful words, the wedges had gone down the way sand goes through a clock.

“I have one question left,” she said. “The last real one. And I want to ask it honestly.”

The Rind said, “Then ask.”

“I keep wishing for things. Pay the rent. Fix the plan. Block the bulldozer. Wash it away. Every time I get one, and every time something bigger breaks. And you — you keep giving me what I ask for. Exactly what I ask for.” She laid her hand flat on the counter. “What does enough mean? Grandma said it all the time. I never understood. I wish I knew what she meant.”

The Rind went very still.

“That’s the one,” it said softly. “That’s the question she finally asked.”

The wedge lifted itself from the tin and rose into the air, and Maren felt the magic wrap around her like an old shawl.

The vision took her to a table. Long, wooden, scarred with years of plates. Grandma Elena stood at the head of it, younger than Maren remembered, flour on her knuckles, a knife in her hand. Around the table sat an assortment of people: vendors Maren recognized from old photographs, a delivery man, a woman with a crying baby, young Omar behind a stack of papers. Someone was always arriving. Elena always cut another slice.

“How do you do it?” a voice asked — Maren’s own voice, young and thin. “How do you know when you have enough?”

Elena didn’t look up. She was laying cheese and bread across the table, a long line of golden pieces.

“Enough is not a number, mija.” She slid one toward the edge of the table, where an empty chair waited. “Enough is a table. When there’s a place for one more. When you can share what you have and the table still holds. That’s enough. I have enough, because I can share it.”

The vision broke. Maren was back in the dark stall. The Rind sat on the counter, smaller than before, slightly dimmed.

“One wedge left,” it said. “Not for a wish. For a meal.”

“But — you said eight wedges. Seven wishes. The eighth —”

“The eighth is for the table.” The Rind’s voice was gentle. “Your grandmother always knew. The last wedge is never for wanting. It’s for making enough to share.”

Maren looked at the wedge in the tin, then at the sun coming up over the closed market. The fair should have been opening. Instead, the whole block was sealed. Demolition at six.

The Rind said, “You wished to know what enough means. Now you know. That doesn’t tell you how to save the market. It tells you why to try.”

Maren stood slowly. She put the tin in her pocket. She unlocked the stall door — the locks didn’t matter anymore, the market was closed — and she stood in the cold morning, looking at the long stretch of empty plaza where the fair tables were supposed to go.

“One wedge,” she repeated. “A meal. And a table.”

The Rind hummed Elena’s song.

“Now you’re cooking,” it said.
