# Chapter 6: Cheese River

Saturday. The smell of cheese had gone from wonder to catastrophe. The loading dock was an amber swamp. The cheddar bulldozer had sagged into a monstrous, creamy stack with a small sign still visible in its ruined middle: PROGRESS IS A GRILLED CHEESE.

The health inspector had returned with two trucks and a fine. The cheese had gotten into the storm drains. The city’s environmental department had been notified. Maren’s phone was a ledger of increasingly agitated messages.

Priya found her at the edge of the dock at dawn, crouched beside the mess. She sat down on a dry crate. For a long time she said nothing.

“You meant well,” she said finally.

“I meant to fix it. I keep making it worse.”

“The bulldozer’s still blocked. He still can’t get in.”

“He doesn’t need to get in,” Maren said. “He just needs to stand back and let the city do it for him.” She looked at the cheese river, creeping toward the market’s door. “I can fix this. I can wash it away. She’d want me to clean up my own mess.”

Priya read her face. “You’re going to wish on it again.”

“It’s the only way.”

The Rind sat in her jacket pocket, patient as a stone. Maren pulled it out. The wedge was worn, smaller, the rind smooth under her thumb.

“I wish,” she said, “that it would rain and wash all this away.”

The Rind made a small, terrible sound.

It rained. A single, compact thundercloud appeared over the market and emptied itself purely onto the loading dock — exactly onto the dock, not the street, not the roof, not the flower boxes. It was a perfect, merciless rain, operated with the same literal precision as everything else she’d asked for.

The cheese river multiplied. It flooded. It poured into the drains and the drains backed up and the street began to smell like a fondue and then like a leak and then like the kind of heat that makes milk turn.

By noon, the city had posted an official notice in the market’s window. OCCUPANCY REVOKED. NO GATHERING. The health code violation was followed by a structural one: the drainage influx had weakened the foundation of the oldest wall in the block, the one that had stood since 1922, the one Elena’s Table leaned against.

Omar stood beside it, reading the second notice. He took off his glasses. He wiped them very slowly.

“Hargrove filed an imminent hazard declaration,” he said. “Demolition official for Sunday evening. Fair’s closing hour. He’s planned the whole thing around it.”

Around her, vendors gathered in tense clusters. Some voices were sharp. Some were quiet. Someone said she could have just thrown the cheese away. She had tried. She had tried that Monday, and the cheese had rolled back to her, and now Maren sat on the curb while a river of ruined dairy inched toward the market’s open doorway.

“Two wedges left,” the Rind said.

“I know.”

“You’re thinking about a wish.”

The thought was rising in her like bile. I wish I could save the market. I wish the market was safe. I wish — she started to say it out loud, and stopped.

The Rind was watching her. Its voice, when it came, was curious. “Go on. I love this part.”

Maren’s hand stayed at her mouth. She did not say the words.

“Not yet,” she said. “I need to understand first.”
