# Chapter 9: The Almost-Wish

The sheriff was a patient woman named Deputy Correa. She stood by her car, arms folded, and read the injunction at the crowd of vendors like a teacher reading the weather.

“I understand,” she said, not unkindly. “But you can’t hold a public gathering on this block. The owner filed a formal order. I have to enforce it.”

The plaza had grown quiet. The candles were unlit. The soup pot sat steaming in the middle of the table, and the table — the whole beautiful plank table Nico had built — stood empty. The vendors who had started to arrive had stopped at the edge of the plaza, watching the sheriff’s car as if it were a wall.

Then Maren heard the voice. It came from her pocket. Steady and old, the Rind’s voice was barely above a breath.

“One wedge left. One word. You could end this now. You could wish for the injunction to vanish, the demolition to stop, the whole thing to be legal. One sentence, and it’s over. You don’t have to trust anyone. You don’t have to ask.”

The wedge was in Maren’s hand. She felt its weight, its golden warmth, the half-inch of bread in her pocket. One word. The final wish was in her throat, beating like a second heart.

“I wish —” she said.

The Rind waited.

Maren looked up the plaza at the vendors. At Nico standing beside the table. At Priya, with her tea cart waiting. At Omar, holding a folder of papers like a shield.

“What would Grandma do?” Maren asked.

The Rind’s voice was very soft. “She would set the table.”

Maren put the wedge back in the tin. She closed the lid.

“I’m not wishing,” she said, and the words sounded strange and strong in the quiet plaza. “I’m setting the table.”

She picked up the soup pot — heavy, burn-scarred — and carried it out to the table herself. Deputy Correa watched. Maren set the pot down. She lit the first candle.

“Nobody leaves this market hungry,” she said.

Behind her, the narrow sound of a match striking. Priya had lit her tea cart’s lantern. Omar lit the second candle. Nico pulled out a chair with a scrape of wood and sat down at the table, and then another vendor came, and another, and then the crowd was moving — not pushing, just flowing — past the sheriff’s car, which had become a piece of scenery, and into the plaza where the table waited.

“The injunction,” Deputy Correa said, but she didn’t unfold her arms. She just stood there, watching the candles come alive, and then she said, almost to herself, “I’m not on duty until six.”

The table began to fill.

Maren’s pocket hummed. The Rind was singing Elena’s song. Not the melody of the vision — the one she had hummed while she cooked, the one that meant the bread was almost ready.

“You almost wished,” it said.

“I know.”

“It would have worked. It would have fixed everything.”

“Then I wouldn’t have learned anything.” Maren ladled soup into a bowl and set it at an empty place. “And the market would have been saved tomorrow by the same woman who broke it yesterday. That’s not enough. That’s just more of the same.”

The Rind was quiet. Then it said, “She would be proud, mija,” and the old word made Maren’s eyes sting.

The table was full. The whole market came, and the plaza filled with the smell of soup and candles and the sound of chairs being pulled out for people who had nowhere else to sit. Maren looked down the long table and saw place settings, and the empty chair at the end, and she set another bowl in front of it anyway.
