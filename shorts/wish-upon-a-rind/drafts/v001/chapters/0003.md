# Chapter 3: A Very Perfect Plan

Wednesday morning, Maren woke with her eyes swollen and a plan-shaped space in her head where hope was supposed to go. She got out of bed, unwrapped the Rind from its napkin — it had somehow migrated from her jacket to the kitchen counter overnight — and looked at it for a long time.

“One more,” she said. “I know what I’m doing this time.”

“You said that yesterday.”

“I wish I had a perfect plan to save the market.”

The Rind’s rind rippled. A long pause. Then it began to hum, and a sheet of paper rolled out of the tin like a tongue of golden-edged parchment, covered in handwriting Maren recognized as her grandmother’s.

Twelve steps.

Step one: Reopen the stall. Step two: Cook Elena’s menu. Step three: Rename nothing. Step four: Invite everyone. Step five: Find the photograph. Step six: Serve the menu at Friday’s anniversary supper. Step seven through eleven: specifics on dough, butter, volunteers, the tea cart’s position, and one folding table that had been in the market’s storage since 1987.

Step twelve: Serve the Rind to the one who needs it.

Maren read it twice. “What does that mean?”

“It means the plan ends where I begin,” the Rind said. “That’s the part you won’t like. That’s the part Grandma Elena liked best.”

The stall opened at nine a.m. Maren scrubbed the shelves, replaced the flaking sign, wiped down the scale that still had rust in the measuring cup. Omar brought a box of her grandmother’s recipe cards. Priya showed up with tea and a griddle and an opinion about the sandwich construction. By noon, the smell of browning butter and toasted bread was drifting down the market’s central aisle, and people who hadn’t walked that way in years were slowing down to sniff.

At four o’clock, Maren found the photograph.

It was boxed behind the pickle barrels, the same place the tin had been — a frame so dusty it looked like it had grown a coat. She wiped it with her sleeve. The photo was old, colored in the way pictures were colored in the sixties, a little sun-faded, still bright.

A boy sat at Elena’s counter, maybe eight years old, swinging his legs, a half-eaten grilled cheese in both hands. Behind him, through the window, a sign read: FIRST WEEK FREE. NOBODY STARTS WITH NOTHING EXCEPT A TABLE.

The sign belonged to a stall across the way. A hand-painted dairy sign with a milk can and the name RUTH’S.

Maren stared at the boy’s face. Narrow. Familiar. Growing colder every year, until it became the face in the Herald’s business section, the face behind the demolition notice.

“That’s him,” Maren said. “That’s Rex Hargrove. As a kid. At my grandmother’s counter.”

The Rind, sitting on the counter beside the photograph, made a sound almost like a purr. “Look closer.”

Maren looked. Behind the boy’s head, on the wall of Ruth’s stall, was a smaller handwritten note, tacked to the frame: My grandson promised me the table stays set. — R.H.

The plan was twelve steps long. The plan was going to work. The plan was going to drag Rex Hargrove’s buried past into the sunlight and set it on a plate.

Maren’s stomach dropped. “We can’t use this. This is — this is a private thing. This is his grandmother.”

“You knew he was in the photo?” the Rind asked.

“I didn’t. Did you?”

The Rind was silent for a moment. Then it said, softly, “I knew he was only a boy once. What a waste.”

Maren looked at the photograph, then at the stall, golden and warm and smelling like butter. She tucked the photo under her arm and closed the shop at sunset, and the plan felt like a blade she was carrying without knowing which way it would cut.
