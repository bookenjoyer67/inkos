# Chapter 2: Same Page

Maren told the truth at the Tuesday market meeting because hiding it felt like drowning.

The vendors met in the old loading bay at sunrise, a ring of folding chairs and thermoses. Priya was there, arms crossed, her tea-cart apron still tied. Nico had come straight from his workshop, sawdust in his hair, a level sticking out of his back pocket. Omar ran the meeting from a clipboard older than anyone in the room.

Maren stood in the middle and read the letter out loud. Silence when she finished. Then everyone started talking at once.

“You cashed a demolition check?”

“That’s not fair, someone tricked her —”

“Fair doesn’t matter. Legal does.”

“I wish,” Maren said, too loud, trying to cut through the noise, “that we were all on the same page so you could see this from my side.”

The room went quiet. A single sheet of paper rustled down from above, drifting out of the air like a leaf, then another, then dozens, settling into laps and coffee cups and onto the table in the center.

Every page was identical. A clause added to every lease in the Pine Street Market, retroactive to the date of signing. Maren’s signature at the bottom. Language stating that any tenant consent to relocation shall apply to all co-tenants in the shared block.

Omar picked up his copy. His glasses slid down his nose. He read it twice.

“This says,” he said slowly, “that one tenant’s acceptance binds all tenants. She signed one thing. We’re all on it.”

“But I didn’t sign anything,” Maren said. “I just wished —”

Her hand went to her mouth. The Rind, sitting in her jacket pocket like a very expensive paperweight, made a sound that could only be described as a pleased hum.

“You wished,” it said, into the button of her collar, “and the page found its way into every lease. That’s what literal means, dear. I do so love the part where you remember.”

Priya set her tea down. Her voice was very controlled. “Seven wedges. You said there are seven wedges left. What did you use one on?”

“Rent.”

“You used a wish on rent. Rent you could have borrowed. From me. From people who love you.” Priya shook her head slowly. “But you’re the first tenant who consented. And now we’re all — we’re all standing on the same page with you. Literally.”

Maren’s chest was shrinking. “I’ll fix it. I’ll find a way.”

Omar pulled a folder from his bag, the edges soft with age. He laid it open on the table. Inside, a single yellowed form, stamped PROVISIONAL in faded red ink.

“The Pine Street Market was put on the historic-preservation list in 2009,” he said. “Provisional. Means they flagged us as worth saving, but nobody ever finished the paperwork to activate it. If the listing is activated before demolition, the block can’t be touched.”

“Then let’s activate it.”

“We tried. The application takes a review. A council vote. A living, occupied market to demonstrate community value.” Omar tapped the paper. “And a stall that’s been active within the last ninety days. Your grandmother’s stall has been dark since she died.”

Across the room, Priya’s face changed. “Tran,” she said.

“Who?”

“Council Member Tran. He drinks tea at my cart every morning. Always asks about the market. Keeps wanting to know if the demolition rumors are true.” She looked at Maren with something that was almost a smile. “If we can show him the market is alive — really alive — he could push the review through.”

Maren looked at the dark stall at the end of the row. Grandma Elena’s sign, the flaking gold letters, the empty shelves.

“The stall,” she said slowly. “We reopen it. By Friday. We show them Elena’s Table is still standing.”

Omar slid a key across the table. It was old, brass, worn smooth by decades of turns.

“Reopen the stall by Friday, or the block is theirs.” He looked at Maren over the folder. “Your grandmother would have had opinions about this. So do I. But I’m too tired to give them.”

The meeting dissolved into argument. Maren barely heard it. She took the key, and the cheese in her pocket shifted, and she felt the shape of the wedge pressing against her ribs like a question.

“One per day,” the Rind said softly. “You used today’s already. You’ll have to find another way through the afternoon.”

Maren looked at the key. Then down at the tin. Seven wedges had become six, and the market had become a boat she was sinking from the inside.

“I wish,” she started, then stopped herself. She pulled her hand out of her pocket instead.

“That’s the trick,” the Rind murmured, almost approving. “You’re learning to count the cost. I do love a student.”
