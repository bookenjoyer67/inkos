# Chapter 5: The Cheddar Bulldozer

The bulldozer arrived at nine a.m. Friday on the back of a flatbed truck, yellow paint gleaming, blade lowered like a jaw. It parked at the edge of the loading dock, and Rex Hargrove’s company had already put up a banner: PROGRESS STARTS HERE.

Maren watched it from the stall’s window. The demolition was supposed to be Sunday. The bulldozer was early. That was the point. Hargrove was showing her the shape of the thing coming for them.

“I can’t stop it,” she said.

“Correct,” said the Rind. It was sitting on the counter, next to the register, where she had started leaving it out of pure exhaustion. “You could, however, block it.”

“I wish I could block the bulldozer.”

The wish happened so fast she tasted the words before she heard them. Butter and bread and the sharp tang of something she couldn’t name. The Rind shivered its whole rind and said, “Done.”

There came a scraping sound from the loading dock, a sound like six tons of cream being poured into a mold. The market vendors streamed out of their stalls to stare.

A second bulldozer — or the shape of one — now sat in the loading dock between the real machine and the market’s back door. But this one was cheddar. Bright, ridiculous, magnificent cheddar. The wheels were wheels of aged gouda. The blade was a slab of something so sharp-smelling the air fizzed. The cab was a hollowed round of smoked cheese with a sign propped in its window: PROGRESS IS A GRILLED CHEESE.

It was six feet tall and forty feet long and completely immovable. The real bulldozer could not get past it. The loading dock was blocked. Hargrove’s crew stood around it with their mouths open.

“That’s cheese,” one of them said. “That’s a cheese bulldozer.”

“That’s magic,” Priya said, and her voice was almost enjoying it. Almost.

Maren’s phone started buzzing. Then Priya’s. Then Omar’s. The cheddar bulldozer was everywhere. Someone had filmed it from the roof across the street. The video went from zero to forty thousand views in an hour.

“Viral,” Nico said, joining her at the dock’s edge. “That’s something — that’s really something — is that cheese waving?”

The cheddar bulldozer was not waving. The heat of the afternoon sun was beginning to soften it. A small amber river, fragrant and butter-bright, started trickling out from under the wheels.

“It’s melting,” Maren whispered.

“It’s cheese,” Nico said. “It’s forty feet of cheese. In the sun.”

The stream spread. The fragrance deepened. Pigeons arrived. Then dogs. Then a health inspector, wearing a mask over her mouth and holding a clipboard like a shield.

“Who is responsible for this?” she asked.

Maren stepped forward. Her phone was still buzzing.

“I need it removed,” the inspector said. “The city’s position is that this constitutes a public health hazard. The drainage situation alone — we can’t have the market’s only loading dock flooding with cheese.”

“It’s blocking the bulldozer,” Maren said. “That’s the point.”

“Then it’s blocking emergency access, too. You have forty-eight hours from this moment to remove it or the city will impound the whole block.” The inspector looked around, and for a moment her professional mask slipped. “This is the most absurd thing I have seen in fifteen years. Please tell me you have a plan.”

Maren looked at the cheddar bulldozer, magnificent and drooling. Then at the tin in her pocket.

“Three wedges left,” the Rind said softly. “And the clock is running.”

The melting bulldozer groaned, shifted, and shed a warm river of cheese across the stones. Maren watched the stream come toward the market like the beginning of the flood she deserved.
