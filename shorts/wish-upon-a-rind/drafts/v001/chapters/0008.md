# Chapter 8: Set the Table

“I’m not asking for a wish,” Maren said into the silence of the loading dock meeting that afternoon.

The vendors stood in a loose ring, some with arms crossed, some just watching. The cheese was still everywhere. The occupancy notice was still on the door. The fair was supposed to have been this morning, and instead the plaza stood empty, and Hargrove’s banner still said PROGRESS STARTS HERE.

“I’m asking for help,” Maren said. “Real help. The kind that doesn’t cost a wedge.”

She explained the plan. The supper that should have been the fair. A long table on the plaza, open to everyone, serving the last of Elena’s bread and her tomato soup and whatever anyone brought to share. She wasn’t asking for permission — the market was closed. She was asking for the table itself.

The Rind, in her pocket, stirred. “I could fix the injunction,” it whispered. “One crumb of bread. One word. I could make the notice vanish. I could —”

“No,” Maren said out loud. The vendors looked at her. “No more wishes. I’m done fixing things with magic.”

Priya was the first to move. She unpacked her tea cart from under a tarp in her stall, despite the notice, because the notice had been revoked and she was tired of missing the fair. Omar found the long fold-up table from storage, the one the plan had mentioned, and dragged it onto the plaza. Nico brought sawhorses and planks of good wood and leveled the whole thing in the time it took Maren to blink.

“I don’t know what to serve,” Maren said. “I don’t know if anyone will come.”

“You cook the soup,” Priya said. “I’ll handle the tea. We’ll find the rest.”

“That’s magical thinking,” Maren said.

“No,” Priya said, and for the first time since Monday, she smiled for real. “That’s a potluck.”

Nico finished the last plank and straightened up. He stood with his shoulder just a fraction too close to hers.

“Your grandmother’s sign,” he said. “The one about the table. Did she really mean it like that? That everyone gets a place?”

“She meant it exactly like that.”

He nodded. Then, very quietly, he said, “There’s a chair for me at this one?”

Maren looked at him. Sawdust in his hair. Hands that had leveled a table she was starting to believe would actually hold. “There’s a chair for everyone tonight, Nico. That’s the whole point.”

The table went up. The candles went out of Omar’s old tin. The soup started simmering in the stall’s original pot, the one that had been there since 1962, the one Elena called her oldest friend.

Then Ms. Fenn arrived with a folded document, and the game changed. Hargrove Development had filed an injunction against public gatherings on the block. The dinner was now illegal. Hargrove was throwing his own event at the fair’s closing hour — champagne for investors at five, and the sledgehammer at six. The injunction had been filed against the dinner specifically.

“He’s not trying to stop you,” Ms. Fenn said, standing back from the table. “He’s trying to make sure no one is here to see it when it goes.”

She left the injunction on the table and walked away.

The vendors stood around the long plank table. The candles were unlit. The soup was hot. And a sheriff’s car pulled up to the plaza just as the first vendor reached for a lighter.
