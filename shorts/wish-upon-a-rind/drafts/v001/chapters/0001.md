# Chapter 1: Out of Crumbs

Maren screamed. The cheese did not scream back, which somehow made it worse.

It sat on a small wooden board inside the tin, a wedge of something pale gold and speckled, with a rind so dark it looked almost black. It had the shape of a slice of pie and the mild, unimpressed expression of a man waiting for a bus.

“You’re not real,” Maren said.

The cheese sighed. “I am the most real thing in this room, child. Please close the tin. The light is harsh.”

She slammed the lid. Counted to ten. Opened it again.

“Better,” said the cheese. “My name is the Rind. Your grandmother made me. She had a gift for fermentation and a terrible habit of keeping promises. You are Maren. You are about to lose this stall, and your cart went through a pothole yesterday, and your rent is due in nine days. Sit down and listen.”

Maren sat down because her legs made the decision for her. The stall swam around her — the dusty shelves, the old scale, the sign that still read ELENA’S TABLE in flaking gold letters.

“I grant one wish,” the Rind said. “One wish per day. A wedge must be eaten with bread. The wish will be granted literally, permanently, and with my personal mischief considered. Eat me. Wish. See what happens.”

“Grandma left me a talking cheese.”

“Grandma left you a way out. You were always her stubborn one. She knew you’d find the tin only when you’d run out of other options. And look at you, Maren. Crumbs everywhere.”

The worst part was the gentleness in its voice. The worst part was how much it sounded like Elena.

Maren had two dollars in her pocket. Her grilled-cheese cart had a cracked axle and a wheel that wobbled like a loose tooth. The rent was nine days away and the demolition notice was folded in her pocket and she had spent the morning scrubbing old grease off shelves that weren't even hers anymore.

She found a heel of bread in her bag. It was two days old. It was enough.

She ate the wedge with the bread, and the cheese tasted like the year her grandmother taught her to flip a sandwich, and Maren swallowed the whole of it and said, without thinking, “I wish I could pay the rent.”

The tin went quiet.

“Wonderful,” said the Rind. “That’s going to be fun.”

There was a knock at the stall door. Maren opened it to find no one. On the ground sat a white envelope with her name on it, the paper so crisp it could have cut her.

Inside was a check for exactly two months’ rent. Thirteen hundred dollars, written in careful blue ink. A letter behind it explained that the Pine Street Market had been selected for relocation assistance, and that accepting the funds constituted the tenant’s official consent to the demolition schedule.

“Consent,” Maren said, reading it again. The word tasted wrong.

“You wished to pay the rent,” the Rind said pleasantly. “You didn't wish to be smart about it.”

She heard heels on the concrete before she saw the woman. She was tall, precise, wearing a gray suit that probably cost more than Maren’s whole cart. She smiled with the warmth of a calculator.

“Ms. Vasquez,” she said. “I’m Ms. Fenn, from Hargrove Development. I see you’ve received our welcome package.”

“You can’t just — this is a trap. I didn’t know what I was signing.”

“You didn’t sign anything. You cashed it, or you will. Either way, the form is binding. It’s the most standard language in the industry. Congratulations.”

Maren’s hands shook as she read the fine print. “Hargrove,” she said. “Rex Hargrove owns this block?”

“Mr. Hargrove has owned this block for four years,” Ms. Fenn said. “The market is the last undeveloped parcel in the corridor. The demolition is scheduled for next month. Your grandmother’s stall is the first one to consent. The countdown has begun.”

She turned and walked away, heels clicking against the concrete like a countdown itself.

Maren stood in the dark stall for a long time. Then she picked up the tin and hurled it toward the dumpster at the end of the loading dock.

It clattered against the metal. There was a pause. Then the tin rolled back, bumped her shin, and tipped over. The Rind sat on the ground, completely unbothered.

“Seven wedges left,” it said. “Seven wishes. Seven more problems. Would you like a moment, or shall we get on with the week?”
