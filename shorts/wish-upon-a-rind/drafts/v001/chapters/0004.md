# Chapter 4: Reason Has a Jingle

Thursday morning, Maren began to believe the plan could work. The stall had customers. Real ones. People who remembered her grandmother’s tomatoes and her pepper butter, people who came back with friends. Priya’s tea cart did double business on the strength of the smell alone. Even Nico stopped by with a repaired wheel for her cart, and he stayed long enough to level a shelf, and he left with a grilled cheese in one hand and an expression Maren refused to name.

“One piece of the puzzle,” she said to the Rind that evening, tucking the photograph into the frame that would hang by the stall’s small window during Friday’s supper. “And if he sees it — if Rex sees it — maybe he remembers. Maybe he changes his mind.”

“And if he doesn’t?” the Rind asked.

“Then I’ll make him see reason.”

The Rind made a considering sound. “Your grandmother used to say that a person convinced against their will is of the same opinion still. But I did always enjoy watching the attempt.”

Maren should have heard the warning. She didn’t.

“I wish,” she said, before she could talk herself out of it, “that Rex Hargrove would listen to reason.”

The wish took the form of a jingle. Maren felt it leave her like a note sung into the air, a silly three-note melody, and she watched it go with a flicker of dread that arrived at the same moment as the Rind’s almost-smile.

“Well,” the Rind said. “He’ll be humming that all night. Let’s see if he finds it reasonable.”

The anniversary supper was beautiful. The photo by the window cast the whole room in a warm, dangerous light.

Rex Hargrove arrived at seven in a suit worth more than every cart in the market combined. Ms. Fenn walked one step behind him. The crowd parted. He looked around the stall — at the counter, the menu board, the jar of pepper butter — and for a moment, Maren watched something soften behind his eyes.

Then he saw the photograph.

His face changed. Maren saw the exact instant the memory turned from something warm into a wound. He crossed the room, plucked the frame off the hook, held it up to the light. His jaw was tight.

“Where did you get this?”

“It was in the stall. It’s the market’s history.”

“It’s private.” His voice was low and low-temperature. “That’s my grandmother. That’s her stall. And you’ve put it up like a — like a prop. Like a souvenir of a past that has nothing to do with this.”

“It has everything to do with it,” Maren said, and her hand was shaking but she kept it steady. “You sat at that counter. You made a promise. If you could just remember —”

“I remember,” Hargrove said, and the word tasted like gravel. “I remember that being poor was a story people told about the block so they could feel better about themselves. I remember that goodwill doesn’t pay bills.”

He set the photograph back on the hook — hard enough that the glass cracked. Maren flinched.

“Demolition,” he said, very quietly, “is moved to Sunday. The market’s fair goes on as planned. Champagne at five, sledgehammer at six. The fair will be the last thing this block remembers.”

He turned and walked out. Ms. Fenn paused at the door long enough to give Maren a look that was almost kind.

“You found the one thing that would turn him stubborn,” the lawyer said. “The past. Congratulations. You lost.”

The stall was quiet after he left. The grilled cheese was going cold. Maren stood very still, listening to the jingle that had wrapped itself around Rex Hargrove’s brain like a worm on a hook, taking care to sound completely reasonable.

“I didn’t make him listen,” she said.

“No,” the Rind agreed. “You reminded him what he buried. Now he has to kill it before it grows. It’s an old magic, dear. You did it to yourself.”

Maren picked up the cracked photograph. A boy at a counter. A promise on the wall. Four wedges left, and the demolition arrived in three days.
