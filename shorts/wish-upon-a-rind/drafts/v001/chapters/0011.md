# Chapter 11: The Midnight Review

The council meeting was moved to the market plaza at midnight, because that was where the table was, and because Council Member Tran had been sitting at it since seven.

“The provisional preservation listing,” Tran said, standing beside Priya’s tea cart with a microphone borrowed from a busker, “can be activated if the market demonstrates active community use and support. You have demonstrated both. The question is whether we, as a council, have the spine to honor it.”

The live-stream went out. The lights were on. The whole block was lit, packed with people.

Rex Hargrove sat at the table with his hands flat on the wood. Ms. Fenn stood behind his chair, reading the emergency review documents on a tablet. The sledgehammer had been moved to the sidelines. It still leaned against the market’s wall, but it looked like a piece of the scenery now.

“This block is a liability,” Hargrove said when the council asked for his position. His voice was tired, but still fighting. “Foundation damage from the flood. Health code violations. A melting structure. You can’t protect a building that’s crumbling around you.”

“The foundation damage was caused by magic cheese,” Tran said mildly. “Which is the first time I’ve ever written those words in an interdepartmental memo. It can be repaired. The flood was clean dairy. The melt was contained to the loading dock. What cannot be repaired,” he said, and his voice dropped, “is the market itself, if we let it be demolished.”

Ms. Fenn leaned forward. “Mr. Hargrove has offered concessions —”

“He’s offered nothing,” Tran said. “He’s filed to dismantle a historic block. The council will now vote to activate the provisional listing, which protects the block from demolition, indefeasibly, as long as the market remains actively serving the community.”

Maren held her breath. The vote was three to one. The minute it came through, the plaza erupted.

“The listing is activated,” Tran said, and he was almost smiling. “The demolition permit is void.”

Hargrove said nothing. He sat at the table, staring at the open ledger in front of him. Ruth’s note. Rex promised to keep the table set.

Maren didn’t push him. She served the last of the soup to a vendor who had missed it. She watched the council begin the paperwork.

An hour later, she found Hargrove alone at Ruth’s stall.

He was standing in front of the shuttered door, his hand on the handle. Not opening it. Just touching it. The old cracked sign, the milk can, the dark windows. Maren walked over, said nothing, and set a plate on the board outside the stall. Bread, cheese, a small dish of pepper butter.

“What’s that for?” Hargrove asked.

“For Ruth.” Maren stepped back. “I leave it every night now. I started when you moved the demolition to Sunday. I thought she deserved to have a place set.”

Hargrove looked at the plate. His hand came off the door handle. It hung at his side, and then it went up to his face, and when he lowered it, his hand was shaking.

“Her first week,” he said, and his voice was raw. “The stall was empty. No customers. Elena came over with bread and cheese and said, First week free. Nobody starts with nothing except a table. She didn’t let my grandmother pay for a month.”

“I know,” Maren said.

“I promised her.” His voice cracked. “I promised her I would keep the table set.”

“You did keep it for a while.”

“And then I bought the block.” He looked over at her. There was no anger in his face now, just the shape of a man doing arithmetic on a long-delayed debt. “I had the permit served. I made the speeches. I moved the demolition to the fair. I was going to serve champagne where she served bread.”

“You can still change things,” Maren said.

“The listing is activated. There’s nothing I can change. The council voted.”

“The listing protects the block. It doesn’t require you to love it.” Maren stepped closer. “You can’t demolish it. But you could still own it. You could still hold it in trust and watch it rot. That would be worse than breaking it.”

Hargrove looked at the plate again. The bread. The cheese. The pepper butter in the small jar, the recipe she had learned from Elena.

“A ninety-nine-year lease,” he said, and he said it like he’d been carrying it around for hours, waiting for the moment it could be said. “One dollar a year. Community trust owns the market; the company holds the lease. But the Vasquez stall stays the anchor. That’s the term. That’s the only term I want.”

“You’re giving it away,” Ms. Fenn said quietly, from behind them.

“I’m keeping the table set,” Rex Hargrove said.

From Maren’s pocket, the Rind said very softly: “One wedge left. Tonight, we serve it.”
