# Chapter 10: The Dinner Stays

Rex Hargrove arrived at six with a bottle of champagne in one hand and a sledgehammer over his shoulder. Ms. Fenn walked behind him, carrying a briefcase.

He stopped at the edge of the plaza. The long table was full. Candles. Soup. Bread in baskets. Children drawing on the ground with chalk. A string of market-lights that someone had found in storage, strung from the lampposts, glowing like a row of small suns.

“This is a gathering,” Hargrove said. His voice sounded strange. “There’s an injunction.”

“Good evening, Mr. Hargrove,” Maren said. She had been watching for him. She was calm in the way a person is calm after making peace with something. “We saved you a seat.”

She pulled out the chair at the head of the table. The empty one. The one she had set a bowl in front of.

“You brought champagne,” Priya said. “That’s festive. We’ll use it for the soup.”

Hargrove didn’t move. The sledgehammer stayed on his shoulder.

“I’m not here to sit down,” he said. “I’m here to —” He stopped, because he had seen the photograph.

It wasn’t by the window where he’d cracked it. Someone had taken the frame down, repaired the glass, and propped it at the center of the table, next to the soup pot. A boy at a counter. A sign in the background. A promise on the wall.

“The demolition,” Hargrove said, and his voice was thinner, “is scheduled.”

“The market’s been closed since yesterday,” Maren said. “The city revoked occupancy. You don’t need to demolish anything tonight. The block’s already dead.”

“Then why —” he gestured with the hammer, “— are you having dinner in a corpse?”

“Because the table’s still warm.” Maren stepped toward him, not too close. “That’s what Elena used to say. The table’s still warm, so the house is still a home.”

Hargrove’s jaw tightened. He looked away from the table, at Ruth’s stall across the plaza, dark and shuttered. The sign was faded. The milk can had a crack in it.

“I’m going to open this market with a sledgehammer,” he said, “and you’re going to watch me do it, and then —”

Omar stepped forward. He was holding an old ledger, brown leather, the edges worn soft as cloth. He laid it on the table, in the candlelight, and opened it.

“This was in the archives,” Omar said. “I almost threw it out in ’99.”

Hargrove looked down. His name was there — REX, in a child’s printing, in the margin of a page dated fifty years ago. Ruth Hargrove’s stall. First week free. Sponsorship by Elena Vasquez. And a note in Ruth’s handwriting, neat and careful:

Rex promised to keep the table set.

Hargrove stared at the page. The sledgehammer lowered. It rested on the ground, and Maren realized he was no longer holding it like a tool; he was holding it like a crutch.

“That’s my grandmother’s handwriting,” he said, and his voice cracked on the last word.

From Maren’s pocket, the Rind spoke so softly only she could hear. “Ruth would have wanted a seat at this table.”

“Mr. Hargrove,” Ms. Fenn said, a sharp professional note. “The demolition is scheduled. The ceremony —”

“Champagne at five,” the Rind was still whispering, “sledgehammer at six. Your grandmother would have offered the champagne to the table.”

Maren pulled out the chair at the head of the table again.

“Sit down, Rex,” she said. “There’s soup. There’s bread. There’s a photograph of you when you were eight and still believed in things.” She set a bowl in front of him and stepped back. “That’s all this is. A dinner. You can eat it and go break the market, and I’ll stand here and watch, and I won’t stop you.”

“You should have left this buried,” Hargrove said, and his voice was not angry anymore.

“I didn’t dig up the past, Rex. I set a place for it.”

He looked from the photograph to Ruth’s stall to the long table. His fingers found the edge, the wood, the grain — the same sawdust and care that had gone into the table all the vendors had helped build. Maren watched him find the edge of the table like a man looking for the shape of a promise.

Then he sat down.

The plaza was silent for a full beat. Then Priya said, very steadily, “I’m putting your champagne in the soup,” and the laughter broke the moment open like bread.
